Assessment season

We made it through another assessment season

Protest hearings are complete, and the county board has finished its equalization work.

Some owners received the changes they asked for. Some received partial changes. Some properties remained as assessed, and some cases may continue to the Nebraska Tax Equalization and Review Commission. That range is fairly typical, but every result matters to the owner involved. Before turning to budgets and levies, it is worth looking at what this year's work accomplished—and at what the history of one real home can teach us.

Equalization

By the measures that matter, we moved in the right direction

By the measures used to evaluate equalization, Gage County moved in the right direction this year. The overall relationship between residential assessments and the market improved. That does not mean the work is perfect or finished, but the completed revaluation left the residential roll in a better statistical position than it was a year ago.

Technical equalization detail

From 2025 to 2026, the residential median ratio moved from 94.45 to 96.44, the coefficient of dispersion improved from 24.42 to 17.03, and the price-related differential moved from 1.077 to 1.041. The PRD improved but remains just above the IAAO reference range of 0.98 to 1.03.

A note on Nebraska law

Nebraska real property is assessed each year as of January 1. Separately, state law requires a systematic inspection and review program that reaches every parcel at least once every six years. The six-year requirement is an inspection-and-review requirement, not a six-year assessment cycle.

Neb. Rev. Stat. § 77-1301·Neb. Rev. Stat. § 77-1311.03

Those countywide measures tell us whether the roll moved in the right direction overall. Property owners, of course, experience that work one home at a time.

I don't want to use one home's history to dismiss what anyone else has experienced. Every property is different, every tax district is different, and an increase that looks manageable on paper may still matter a great deal within a household budget.

But this is a recognizable Beatrice example: an ordinary home listed in average condition, with no major addition or new construction identified in the current record. It cannot tell us what happened everywhere. It can help us see one important distinction clearly.

A percentage change in assessed value is not, by itself, a forecast of the percentage change in the final tax bill.

How the bill is built

How an assessment becomes a tax bill

Here is the important distinction: an assessment is one input in a property-tax bill. It is not the bill itself.

  1. Assessment

    Establishes taxable value and the property's relative share.

  2. Budgets

    Establish the revenue local public bodies request.

  3. Levies

    Apply those revenue needs across the taxable value in each district.

  4. Final tax bill

    Combines the levies for the property's county, city or village, school, and other applicable districts.

Exemptions and credits can change what an individual owner ultimately pays.

That is why a larger valuation base can allow a levy to fall when budget needs do not grow at the same pace. It can—but it does not guarantee that it will.

That explains how assessments and tax bills can move differently. It leaves another question: now that the countywide revaluation is complete, what might the market require next?

Market context

The revaluation crossed an unusually turbulent period

The revaluation crossed a volatile post-pandemic sales period. Nationally, very low mortgage rates and strong demand accompanied rapid housing activity and price movement. Higher rates later restrained activity, and the Federal Reserve reported in July 2026 that existing-home sales had been moving sideways at relatively low levels for several years. That is national context, not a Gage County forecast.

My opinion—not a forecast

This is my opinion: Higher borrowing costs appear to be keeping the market from moving at the pace we saw several years ago. If that continues, I think Gage County may be positioned for a more ordinary assessment cycle next year—one driven by sales in particular locations and property groups rather than another broad countywide reset.

That does not mean values will be flat, and it is not a promise about any individual property. It is simply my reading of where the market appears to be today.

Whatever the market does next, the 2026 values are now largely set. The decisions still ahead this year belong to budgets, property-tax requests, and levies.

Those are the general questions. Here in Gage County, several specific needs are already part of the conversation.

Matters to watch

A few local decisions remain unsettled

Beatrice's street plan keeps road and infrastructure needs in the budget conversation. The county's law-enforcement center remains a proposal, and its financing has not been settled; possible approaches include a voter-approved bond or legislative authority for a targeted sales tax. School budgets will remain a major part of many local tax bills, but I have not found a sufficiently concrete new Beatrice school bond or facility proposal to include here.

Those budget and financing decisions will play out over the coming months. At the same time, the assessment calendar does not stop.

Looking toward 2027

Less like a reset, more like annual maintenance

Annual assessment work does not stop when a countywide revaluation ends. The 2027 assessment date arrives January 1. Sales will still be reviewed, records will still be maintained, and market evidence may still require adjustments.

With the countywide revaluation complete, the next few years may feel less like a major reset and more like routine annual maintenance—provided the market remains reasonably steady. Not necessarily flat, and never automatic. But hopefully less turbulent.
